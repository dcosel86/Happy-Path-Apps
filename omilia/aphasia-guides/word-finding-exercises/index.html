Word-finding exercises for aphasia: 8 to try at home

You know exactly what you want to say, but the word won’t come. That’s one of the most common — and most frustrating — parts of aphasia. The good news: word-finding is a skill you can practice. Here are eight exercises you can do at home, plus tips for caregivers who want to help.

What is word-finding difficulty?

Word-finding difficulty — also called anomia — is trouble coming up with the right word, especially names for people, places and things. It can feel like a constant “tip of the tongue” moment. Someone might:

  • pause for a long time searching for a word,
  • use a related word instead (“fork” for “spoon”),
  • describe the thing instead of naming it (“the thing you drink coffee from”), or
  • use a general word like “thing” or “stuff.”

Word-finding problems show up in nearly every type of aphasia, whether it follows a stroke, a brain injury or another condition. When it’s the main symptom, it’s sometimes called anomic aphasia.

Before you start

  • Check in with your speech-language pathologist (SLP). They can tell you which exercises fit your goals and how hard to make them.
  • Keep sessions short and regular. Ten to twenty focused minutes most days usually beats one long, tiring session a week.
  • Aim for success. Practice should be challenging but doable. If most attempts end in frustration, make it easier.
  • Use words that matter. Family names, favorite foods and places you go are more useful — and more motivating — than random vocabulary.
  • Stop when tired. Fatigue makes word-finding harder. It’s fine to end on a good attempt and come back later.

Eight word-finding exercises to try

1. Picture naming with cues

Look at a picture of an everyday object and try to name it. If the word doesn’t come, a helper gives cues, starting with the smallest hint and only giving more if needed:

  1. Meaning cue: “You use it to eat soup.”
  2. Sentence cue: “Pass me the salt and the pepper and a…”
  3. First-sound cue: “It starts with ‘sp…’.”
  4. Say it together, then try to say it alone.

Try it: pick 10 pictures of things in your kitchen. Name each one, then try the whole set again later in the day.

2. Describe it: features first

Instead of jumping straight to the name, talk about the object’s features: What group does it belong to? What is it used for? Where would you find it? What does it look like? What does it remind you of? Thinking through these features often brings the word with it. SLPs use a structured version of this called semantic feature analysis, which is one of the most studied word-finding techniques.

Try it: for “apple” — it’s a fruit, you eat it, it grows on trees, it’s red or green, it’s crunchy. Then say the word.

3. Category naming

Set a timer for one minute and name as many things as you can in a category: animals, foods, clothing, things in a bathroom. Write down the count and try to beat it another day.

4. Finish the sentence

Familiar phrases give the brain a running start. A helper says the beginning and you finish it: “You brush your… teeth.” “Bread and… butter.” “Salt and… pepper.”

5. Talk around the word

Sometimes the most useful skill in a real conversation is getting your meaning across even when the exact word won’t come. Practice describing things without naming them: “the big yellow vehicle that takes kids to school.” This strategy is called circumlocution, and it keeps conversations moving.

6. Write it, draw it or gesture it

For some people, writing the first letter, sketching the object or acting out how it’s used helps the spoken word come. Even when it doesn’t, it’s another way to communicate.

7. Say it, then use it

Once you find a word, use it right away in a short sentence: “Spoon. I need a spoon.” Using new words in context helps them stick, and it’s closer to how words are used in real conversation.

8. Build a personal word list

Make a list — or a deck of photo cards — of the words that matter most in daily life: the names of family members, pets, medications, favorite places and foods. Practice these first; they’re the ones you’ll use every day.

How caregivers can help

  • Give time. Wait a little longer than feels natural before stepping in. Many words arrive after a pause.
  • Offer cues, not answers. Start with a small hint, like what the thing is used for, before giving the first sound or the whole word.
  • Ask what they prefer. Some people want you to supply the word quickly; others want to find it themselves. Ask, and respect the answer.
  • Keep it relaxed. Practice works best when it doesn’t feel like a test. Avoid quizzing during stressful moments or in front of others.
  • Celebrate effort. Every attempt is practice, and small wins add up.

Frequently asked questions

Can word-finding improve years after a stroke?

Many people keep making progress for months and even years after a stroke, especially with regular practice. Progress is different for everyone, so a speech-language pathologist is the best person to set realistic goals.

How often should you practice word-finding exercises?

Short, regular sessions tend to work best, such as 10 to 20 minutes on most days. Your speech-language pathologist can recommend a schedule that fits your goals and energy levels.

Is anomia the same as aphasia?

Not exactly. Anomia means difficulty finding words, and it is a symptom of almost every type of aphasia. When word-finding difficulty is the main symptom, it is sometimes called anomic aphasia.

Should I finish sentences for someone with aphasia?

Give them time first, and offer small hints before giving the whole word. Preferences differ, so ask the person whether they want help finding words and how much.

Are word-finding apps a replacement for speech therapy?

No. Apps like Omilía are designed to support practice at home between sessions, not to replace a speech-language pathologist.
